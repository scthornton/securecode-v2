# SecureCode Web Roadmap Feedback

## Bottom Line

The roadmap is directionally strong, but it is not execution-ready. It is trying to become three things at once: a richer training corpus, a reference benchmark, and a methodology paper. The core risk is not the phase list itself; it is credibility control.

The biggest issues I see:

- The roadmap contains contradictory execution models: newer "single-pass + Scott/Opus review" language coexists with older cross-model-review and agentic sections.
- The v2.2 negatives plan risks silently changing downstream training behavior by folding safe controls into the default split too early.
- The quality gates are still too dependent on one author using one model-assisted review workflow.
- The effort estimates are optimistic, especially for negatives, metadata correctness, detection-rule authoring, and executable verification.
- The strategic adoption risk is not agentic data; it is trust, stable evals, and commercial usability under CC BY-NC-SA.

## Top 3 Changes Before Execution

### 1. Freeze One Source Of Truth

Clean up the roadmap before starting implementation. Right now, `ROADMAP.md` says Phase 4 is executable verification and agentic v3.0 is deferred. `docs/METHODOLOGY.md` still says Phase 4 is agentic v3.0 and executable verification is deferred. The roadmap also says "no automated cross-model review," then later specifies generator/reviewer/adjudicator cross-model review.

This ambiguity will cause execution drift. Pick the architecture, update both documents, and remove stale sections rather than letting contradictory plans coexist.

### 2. Do Not Fold Negatives Into The Default Split Initially

Ship v2.2 safe-but-suspicious negatives as an opt-in `safe_controls` config first. Publish mixing guidance and FP/FN results. Only add negatives to the default train split after evidence shows they improve at least one retrained model without increasing false negatives.

The current Option C default-mixing plan is too aggressive. A user who auto-loads `main` should not silently get a changed training objective for the highest-risk new content type.

### 3. Move Executable Verification Earlier As A Pilot

Do 10-15 gold verified examples before full negatives or before committing to 50 executable verifications. Those examples become calibration anchors for review quality, eval design, and the "validated" claim.

Fifty verified examples is a good eventual target, but too large as the first executable-verification commitment. A smaller pilot will expose the real per-example effort, Docker template limits, CI runtime, and exploit-drift problems.

## Recommended Methodology

I recommend cross-model review for safety-critical generated content, with Scott/Opus as final adjudicator. I do not recommend pure single-pass generation plus Scott/Opus review for v2.2 negatives.

Use this architecture:

```text
Generate
  -> deterministic/schema validation
  -> independent adversarial model review
  -> Scott/Opus adjudication
  -> final validators/eval
```

Recommended gates by phase:

- **Metadata enrichment:** deterministic scripts, source caching, and spot checks. LLMs only for manual edge cases.
- **Safe-but-suspicious negatives:** generator from one provider, cold reviewer from another provider, deterministic validation, then Scott/Opus final review. High-risk patterns need external sample review or should be dropped.
- **Infra examples:** single-pass generation plus Scott/Opus review is acceptable, but detection rules need tool-specific validation where possible.
- **Executable verification:** LLM review is not the primary gate. The primary gate is the exploit/fix harness passing locally and in CI.
- **Agentic:** keep deferred. Do not let agentic requirements shape the current harness unless the phase is re-approved later.

Replace the claim that Scott/Opus "replaces both second human reviewer and automated cross-model review" with something like:

> Automated cross-model review is a triage and adversarial-review layer. Scott/Opus is the final human adjudication layer for accepted safety-critical examples and all disagreements.

For v2.2 negatives, do not ship anything labeled safe unless it passes:

1. Generated by Provider A.
2. Reviewed cold by Provider B for exploitability.
3. Deterministic/schema validation.
4. Scott/Opus final review.
5. External sample review or full extra scrutiny for high-risk patterns.

This preserves human accountability while adding blind-spot coverage and measurable disagreement telemetry.

## 1. Content Scope

### v2.1 Metadata Enrichment

Metadata enrichment is useful, but do not overclaim it. EPSS, CVSS, CAPEC, and ATT&CK mappings are many-to-many and context-sensitive. "100% populated" is less important than "correct enough to filter on."

Concerns:

- CWE-to-ATT&CK and CWE-to-CAPEC derivations can be misleading without scenario context.
- Manual EPSS/CVSS approximation for composite examples may create false precision.
- A breaking OWASP 2025 migration is risky if default consumers expect `owasp_2021`.

Recommendation:

- Preserve legacy OWASP fields.
- Mark derived mappings as derived, not authoritative.
- Add confidence/source metadata for enriched fields.
- Prefer a non-breaking dual-field transition over a default breaking migration.

### v2.2 Safe-But-Suspicious Negatives

This is the highest-value addition, but it is also the highest-risk content.

Some proposed categories are dangerous teaching signals:

- Safe `eval`
- Disabled CSRF
- Permissive CORS
- Self-signed TLS
- Raw SQL with upstream parameterization

These can train models to rationalize bad patterns unless the safety preconditions are enforced in code, not merely explained in prose.

Recommendation:

- Require machine-checkable preconditions where possible.
- Drop categories where safety depends mainly on environmental claims.
- Keep negatives opt-in until measured retraining results exist.
- Track both FP reduction and FN regression.

### Phase 3 securecode-infra

Making infra a sibling dataset is the right decision. Folding IaC and supply chain into SecureCode Web would dilute the dataset's identity.

The scope is still too broad for 90 examples. Terraform, Kubernetes, GitHub Actions, GitLab CI, Jenkins, npm, Cargo, Go modules, container registries, SBOM, provenance, sigstore, and malicious build tooling are not one small domain.

Recommendation:

- For v0.1, narrow to Terraform, Kubernetes, and GitHub Actions.
- Treat broader supply chain as a later expansion.
- Validate detection-rule references against actual tool behavior where feasible.

### v2.4 Executable Verification

This is the strongest differentiator in the roadmap. It should happen, but start smaller.

Recommendation:

- Pilot 10-15 examples first.
- Pick examples that are easy to run locally and cover multiple bug classes.
- Publish the pilot methodology before scaling to 50.
- Avoid implying that "verified" means "secure"; it only means the documented exploit fails against the patched variant.

### What Is Missing

The biggest missing content area is OWASP API Top 10:

- BOLA
- BOPLA
- Broken function-level authorization
- Unsafe API consumption
- Tenant isolation
- Token scopes
- Excessive data exposure
- Pagination and object enumeration

This is more strategically relevant to modern web security than mobile or agentic loops.

Also missing:

- Ordinary safe code
- Medium/low-severity examples
- Boring production examples
- Multi-tenant SaaS authorization patterns
- Cross-service API attack chains

The current dataset is heavily critical/high severity. That creates an unrealistic security prior.

## 2. Methodology

Single-pass generation plus Scott/Opus review is not strong enough for safety-critical generated negatives. It is better than no human review, but it is still one accountable human using one model-assisted workflow.

Failure modes not sufficiently addressed:

- Reviewer anchoring to generated explanations.
- Batch fatigue across 150 examples.
- Shared blind spots between the generator and Opus-assisted review.
- Template-level mistakes replicated across many examples.
- "Safe because of precondition" where the precondition is not enforced.
- Benign GitHub code treated as safe because it has no known CVE.
- Generated prose laundering unsafe patterns into persuasive explanations.
- False-negative regression caused by teaching the model to excuse suspicious code.
- Evaluation leakage from existing SecureCode-trained models selecting the negative taxonomy.
- Licensing/provenance risk from sampled GitHub functions.
- Split contamination by CVE, incident family, framework, or near-duplicate pattern.

Recommended additions:

- External security professional review of at least a 10% sample before v2.2.
- Full human review for any negative category with high model disagreement.
- Pattern-level kill switch: if a pattern cannot be made safe without environmental assumptions, drop it.
- Provenance manifest for every generated example.
- Published reject reasons, not only accepted examples and disagreement rates.

## 3. Effort And Cost Estimates

The cost estimates are plausible only if generation works almost perfectly and review context stays small. That is unlikely.

Likely under-budgeted areas:

- **Phase 1 manual metadata:** 350 manual cases at 2 minutes each is unrealistic. Expect 5-10 minutes each if correctness matters.
- **Phase 2 pattern derivation:** sourcing 500 functions, scoring across 8 models, clustering false positives, and documenting patterns is more than 10 hours.
- **Phase 2 eval:** a 200-safe/200-vulnerable benchmark with leakage controls and repeatability is more than 6 hours.
- **Phase 2 review:** 5 minutes per negative example is too low. Safe-but-suspicious review needs 15-30 minutes for many examples.
- **Phase 3 detection rules:** 6 hours across Checkov, tfsec, Falco, kube-bench, sigstore, OSV, and platform-native controls is not credible.
- **Phase 4 verification:** 80 minutes per executable verification is optimistic. Some Java/Spring, deserialization, SSRF, and auth examples can take half a day or more.
- **CI:** 50 Docker verifications in under 15 minutes is aggressive unless the examples are very small and heavily parallelized.
- **Docs/release QA:** HF configs, Parquet rebuilds, schema migration, changelogs, RC feedback, and user communication are consistently undercounted.

I would reforecast Phases 1-4 at roughly 600-900 hours if the current quality bar remains. If executable verification is reduced to a 10-15 example pilot, 450-650 hours is more realistic.

Also verify model names and pricing before planning API runs. The roadmap references models such as GPT-5.4, Claude Sonnet 4.6, and Claude Opus 4.7. The implementation plan should use confirmed API model IDs and current pricing, not assumed future aliases.

## 4. Sequencing

Recommended order:

1. **Phase 0:** clean roadmap contradictions, schema/version contract, and GitHub/HF source-of-truth reconciliation.
2. **Phase 1:** metadata enrichment with non-breaking legacy fields.
3. **Phase 1.5:** executable verification pilot with 10-15 examples.
4. **Phase 2:** negatives as opt-in `safe_controls` config plus eval.
5. **Phase 3:** infra sibling, narrowed for v0.1.
6. **Phase 4:** scale executable verification to 50 after the pilot proves templates and CI.
7. **Phase 5:** keep agentic deferred.

Parallelization:

- Infra incident research can run while Phase 1 metadata work proceeds.
- Executable verification pilot can run before the full generation harness is finished.
- Negative eval design should happen before negative generation.
- The infra sibling does not need to wait for v2.2 except for shared harness reuse.

Do not let agentic schema or future multi-agent workflows influence the current release architecture.

## 5. Strategic Risks

The biggest strategic risk is not missing agentic data. It is trust plus commercial usability.

SecureCode Web uses CC BY-NC-SA 4.0. That is a blocker for many commercial users. If the goal is to become a reference benchmark with deployment-grade metadata, the license strategy cannot stay out of scope forever.

Recommended strategic work:

- Add a license strategy track: dual license, commercial permission, or benchmark-only terms.
- Publish stable versioning and pinning guidance.
- Maintain immutable benchmark revisions.
- Create a smaller externally audited verified subset.
- Publish reproducible evals, not just dataset growth metrics.

The path to adoption is not just more examples. A smaller, externally reviewed, executable subset may do more for credibility than hundreds of additional synthetic examples.

## Specific Cuts

I would cut or defer:

- Default-split mixing of safe controls until measured evidence exists.
- Agentic schema work until the deferred decision point.
- Broad supply-chain coverage in infra v0.1.
- Any negative category whose safety depends mainly on undocumented environment assumptions.
- Claims that single-author Opus-assisted review is stronger than cross-model review.

## Specific Additions

I would add:

- Phase 0 cleanup and source-of-truth reconciliation.
- Executable verification pilot before v2.2 ships.
- External review sample for v2.2 negatives.
- `safe_controls` opt-in config first.
- API Top 10 as a near-term content strategy.
- Provenance records and reject-reason logs for generated examples.
- Explicit false-negative regression measurement.
- A license/commercial-adoption strategy outside the technical phase list.

## Recommended Roadmap Wording Change

Current problematic framing:

> Scott + Opus review replaces both second human reviewer and automated cross-model review.

Recommended replacement:

> Automated cross-model review is used as a triage and adversarial-review layer for safety-critical generated content. Scott performs final adjudication with Opus assistance for accepted safety-critical examples, all model disagreements, and all high-risk pattern categories. Deterministic validators and release evals remain required final gates.

This is more defensible, more transparent, and less dependent on a single review path.
